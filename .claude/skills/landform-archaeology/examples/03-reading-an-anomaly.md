# Example 3 — Reading a single anomaly

Worked reasoning on one candidate, showing the elimination process. The conclusion is less
important than the order of operations.

## The candidate

Reviewing SLRM at 15 m radius over a wooded slope. A roughly circular positive anomaly,
about 12 m across, maybe 0.3 m of relief. Flat interior. Faintly asymmetric — the upslope
edge looks cut, the downslope edge looks built out.

Tempting first thought: charcoal hearth.

## Work the checklist anyway

**Paired with an adjacent pit?** No. There is no matching depression touching it. Rules
out tree throw, which is the default explanation for anything small and round in mature
forest.

**Closed depression with no spoil?** Not a depression at all — it is positive relief.
Rules out sinkhole, which matters here because this is karst country and sinkholes are the
dominant natural circular feature.

**20–40 m and connected to a trail network?** It is 12 m, which is hearth scale rather
than landing scale. Check for skid trails converging on it in the negative openness band.
Suppose none — nothing connects to it.

**Perfectly straight anything?** No.

**Low ground-return area?** Check canopy. Suppose moderate cover, ground returns present
throughout. Not an interpolation artifact.

**Visible in more than one render?** Positive openness shows a bright ring at the platform
edge. Negative openness shows nothing much, which is what you expect for a feature with no
associated ditch. Two of three — passes.

**Historical aerials and quads?** No structure symbol, no modern clearing, no evidence of
20th-century earthmoving at that spot.

## Now the question that actually decides it

**Is it isolated?**

This is the one people skip, and it is worth more than all the morphology above.

Charcoal hearths do not occur alone. Charcoal was made within economical hauling distance
of a furnace, so a working district contains dozens to hundreds, typically 50–200 m apart,
concentrated on slopes within a few kilometres of the furnace.

So: scan the surrounding slopes for more of the same. Two outcomes.

**If you find fifteen more at similar scale and spacing** — that is a hearth field. The
spatial pattern is doing the identification work, not the individual morphology. Log the
lot as `likely cultural` and map the distribution, because the distribution is the finding.

**If it stays alone after a thorough scan** — downgrade to `ambiguous` regardless of how
good the morphology looked. A single circular platform on a slope with no companions is
more likely a stump mound, a small landing, an old woods road turnout, or a natural bench
than an isolated hearth. The cut-and-fill asymmetry you liked is also what a machine leaves
when it levels anything on a slope.

## The log entry

```
| A-014 | 512340 | 3998120 | 12 | circular platform, cut-and-fill, flat interior |
slrm, opn+ | likely cultural | relict charcoal hearth — one of 16 in a cluster
spanning 400 m on this slope, spacing 60-180 m, consistent with a hearth field |
```

Or, in the isolated case:

```
| A-014 | 512340 | 3998120 | 12 | circular platform, cut-and-fill, flat interior |
slrm, opn+ | ambiguous | hearth morphology but isolated after scanning 1 km of
adjacent slope; stump mound or small landing cannot be excluded |
```

## What made the difference

Not the shape. The context.

Morphology gets you to a shortlist. Spatial pattern, land-use history, and historical
cross-reference are what turn a shortlist into a classification. An anomaly evaluated on
form alone is a guess with extra steps.
